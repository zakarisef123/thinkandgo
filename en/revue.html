Off-market acquisitions: why the best targets are not for sale

The most attractive companies are rarely on the market. Identifying and approaching them takes method, patience and genuine respect for the owner across the table.

When a company is put up for sale through an organised process, it is presented to many buyers at once. Competition drives up the price, the timetable is imposed and the buyer discovers the target through a document designed to show it at its best. For a buyer who knows what they are looking for, this is rarely the best playing field.

The most relevant targets are often family-owned or entrepreneurial companies whose owner has never thought of selling. They appear on no list. To find them, you have to start from the buyer’s strategy, not from the opportunities available.

Starting from what the acquisition must deliver

A market, a technology, production capacity, know-how: every acquisition project answers a specific ambition. Translating that ambition into concrete criteria (size, geography, positioning, culture) produces a shortlist of genuinely compatible companies.

Approaching with tact

An owner who has asked for nothing does not hear a takeover proposal as a sales offer. The first approach must be discreet, personal and respectful. It speaks of an industrial project and continuity before it speaks of price. The quality of that first contact often determines whether a conversation can begin.

What the buyer gains

  • A direct relationship with the owner, without immediate competition.
  • Time to understand the company in depth before taking a position.
  • A target chosen for its strategic fit, not because it happened to be available.

An off-market approach requires more preparation than responding to an organised process. That is precisely what gives it its value.

Selling your business: what to prepare before talking to a buyer

A sale is largely decided before the first meeting. Value, information, timing: what owners gain by planning ahead.

Many owners approach a sale when they receive an expression of interest or when a personal milestone approaches. Yet most of the levers behind a good transaction are worked on well in advance: clear figures, a solid team, a readable business model.

Understanding what creates value for a buyer

A buyer does not only pay for past results. They pay for what the company will enable them to do tomorrow: a market position, loyal customers, a technology, teams. Identifying these elements and being able to demonstrate them is the core of a strategic valuation, well beyond a simple multiple.

Preparing the information

The moment the buyer examines the company in detail is often when value erodes. Incomplete information, missing contracts or a poorly explained dependency fuel doubts and justify price adjustments. A carefully prepared data room, with information shared at the right time with the right person, protects the negotiation.

Choosing the right buyer, not just the highest price

  • What future for the teams and the brand?
  • What role for the owner after the transaction?
  • What guarantees and conditions come with the price?

A successful sale is one the owner is still proud of years later. It begins with discreet preparation, long before the first meeting.

Cross-border deals: culture matters as much as numbers

Between Switzerland, Europe and North America, negotiations are also won by understanding the codes on each side of the table.

A cross-border acquisition brings together two legal systems, two tax regimes and often two very different ways of negotiating. Technical questions are settled with the right experts. Cultural misunderstandings, however, can derail an otherwise well-built transaction.

What is said, and what is not

Attitudes to time, the role of hierarchy, the way disagreement is expressed or concessions are made vary greatly from one country to another. A silence may mean reflection or refusal. A quick answer may signal interest or simple courtesy. Understanding these nuances avoids misreading the other party.

The role of a multicultural adviser

Knowing the languages and local codes makes it possible to translate far more than words: the seller’s real expectations, concerns and what truly matters to them. This is a decisive advantage for a foreign buyer interested in a Swiss company, as for a Swiss company expanding abroad.

  • Preparing each meeting with local practices in mind.
  • Mobilising legal, tax and financial experts in each country.
  • Keeping one negotiating line, whatever the language.

In a cross-border transaction, trust is built in both languages. It is often what makes the difference at the moment of closing.

Valuation: what a buyer really pays for

A multiple does not tell the whole story. A company’s value also lies in what it enables its buyer to do tomorrow.

Earnings multiples are a useful starting point. They give an order of magnitude and make it possible to compare companies. But two companies with similar figures can be worth very different amounts to the same buyer.

Intrinsic value and strategic value

Intrinsic value rests on the company’s ability to generate results on its own. Strategic value depends on the buyer: what the target brings in terms of market, technology, capacity or teams, and what the buyer could not obtain as quickly otherwise.

What tips the balance

  • The quality and recurrence of revenue.
  • Dependence on a few customers, suppliers or key people.
  • Realistic synergies, and the time needed to achieve them.
  • Risks identified during due diligence.

For the buyer, the challenge is to know the fair price to defend and the limit not to exceed. For the seller, it is to demonstrate what makes the company unique. In both cases, a well-argued valuation is the foundation of the negotiation.

Confidentiality: protecting a deal from first contact to closing

A rumour can unsettle clients, teams or competitors. Confidentiality must be protected at every step, not only by a contract.

A merger or acquisition touches what is most sensitive in a company: its figures, its customers, its teams and its future. If word gets out too early, relationships with customers, employee morale or the competitive position may suffer.

An agreement from the first conversation

A non-disclosure agreement signed at the outset sets the framework. It specifies what information is protected, who may access it and what happens to it if discussions end.

Information shared in stages

Not everything is shared at once. General information first, detailed data next, and the most sensitive elements only once the other party’s interest is confirmed. A well-managed data room shows who has consulted what, and when.

Day-to-day discipline

  • Code names for projects.
  • A small circle of informed people.
  • Communication prepared for announcement day.

Discretion is not a procedural detail. It is a condition for the transaction to happen, on good terms for all parties.
